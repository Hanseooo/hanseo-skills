# Product Discovery Principles

These principles are hypotheses and decision aids, not universal laws.

## 1. Understand the decision before judging the product

A weekend experiment, first paid product, long-term independent business, and venture-scale company should not be evaluated using identical standards.

## 2. Search for a reason to win

An empty market is one possible advantage, not the objective.

A product may deserve preference through:
- workflow whitespace;
- a better mechanism;
- craft/output quality;
- timing;
- distribution;
- identity/community fit;
- domain insight;
- integration;
- business-model fit;
- superior defaults or ergonomics.

## 3. Value is broader than pain

People can pay for convenience, desire, delight, identity, expression, aspiration, confidence, control, privacy, professional quality, emotional meaning, or entertainment.

## 4. Look for the magic transition

When possible, define a visible before → after. Strong transformations are often easier to understand, demonstrate, and sell.

## 5. Look for the intervention point

A product may win because it acts at exactly the right moment, not merely because it has more features.

## 6. Measure craft leverage

Exceptional craft matters most when experience, output, trust, or personality changes purchase preference. Craft is not synonymous with visual decoration.

## 7. Product and distribution should reinforce each other

A good product may naturally generate demonstrations, artifacts, recipients, marketplace visibility, search demand, community conversations, or paid-ad-friendly creative.

## 8. Pricing should be congruent with value and cost

Perpetual, subscription, credits, usage, transaction fees, freemium, and one-off payment can all be correct depending on the job.

## 9. Evidence types are not interchangeable

Attention is not activation. Activation is not retention. Retention is not payment. Payment is not healthy economics. Launch success is not durability.

## 10. Validation should scale with the cost of being wrong

A reversible two-day experiment may be best validated by shipping. A months-long high-risk system deserves much stronger validation before implementation.

## 11. Test the riskiest assumption

Do not validate the easiest technical part. Find the uncertainty most capable of killing the product and attack it cheaply.

## 12. Competition is contextual

Competition can prove demand, reveal vocabulary, and show pricing. It invalidates an idea only when the proposed reason-to-win cannot survive the actual alternatives.

## 13. Timing can justify short commercial half-life

A cheap, fast, timely product does not need the defensibility standards of a long-lived SaaS business.

## 14. Founder advantages matter, but do not create demand

Audience, community access, domain expertise, technical preparedness, prior products, and distribution capital change the opportunity set. They do not excuse weak user value.

## 15. Technology is leverage, not automatically a market

Start from what a capability enables users to do better. Avoid turning every new model/API into a wrapper thesis.

## 16. Separate launch quality from product health

Great marketing can coexist with poor reviews, weak retention, pricing problems, or reliability issues.

## 17. Portfolios can compound with role clarity

Products may generate cash, audiences, internal tooling, marketing assets, or capabilities for each other. This is useful only when each product has a defined strategic role.

## 18. Products are allowed to die

Killing an idea early is a successful outcome when it preserves time and focus.

## 19. Avoid cargo culting

Visible traits of winners are not automatically causal or transferable.
